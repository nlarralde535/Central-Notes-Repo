# Houston Chronicle 
Harris County taxpayers, for the first time in recent history, are directly paying for deputies to patrol predominantly wealthy neighborhoods through the county’s contract patrol program, a subsidy that one expert said may violate state law. 

The situation was created by a combination of sweeping raises Commissioners Court granted to county law enforcement last year and a series of state laws Republican lawmakers passed to protect police funding and the popular contract patrol program after Democrats took over county government in 2019. But the subsidies have prompted even one of the program’s strongest supporters in the Legislature to say the laws governing contract deputies must be revisited.

The subsidies, and the law enforcement raises more broadly, are a significant driver in the roughly $180 million deficit commissioners must close at their meeting Thursday, at which they are expected to pass a budget for the coming fiscal year and formally approve a large property tax rate hike they preliminarily approved earlier this month. 

The contract patrol program cost roughly $190.4 million in fiscal year 2026. Of that, county taxpayers covered $76.1 million.

In a typical contract patrol agreement, a neighborhood pays 70% of the cost of a deputy’s salary and the county covers 30%, with the expectation that the deputy spends 30% of his time outside the contract area. 

County administrators do not verify that happens, but view these arrangements as satisfying a state law that mandates the county recover “100 percent” of the costs associated with the contract patrol program. 

Then commissioners in May 2025 voted to increase deputies’ pay roughly 50% over five years. Fearing mass cancellations, commissioners unanimously voted not to pass on the full cost of the raises to neighborhoods with patrol contracts overnight. 

Instead, county taxpayers – including in neighborhoods that cannot afford extra patrols – are to subsidize the program for five years as contract patrol rates gradually rise. In the current budget year, that subsidy is nearly $40 million. The program's total cost to county taxpayers is $76 million, roughly 40% of the cost of the contract patrol program. 

Asked for comment on the subsidies, Harris County Attorney Abbie Kamin pointed to commissioners' initial pledge that neighborhoods with patrol contracts eventually would be asked to retroactively pay the difference between the deputies’ salaries and the subsidized contract rates. 

But commissioners have not outlined a plan for how taxpayers would be made whole, and a slew of contracts submitted for renewal at the court’s Thursday meeting do not mention retroactive payments. 

Commissioners chose to subsidize the contract patrol program rather than risk a wave of cancellations in part due to a state law prohibiting commissioners from “defunding” law enforcement. 

The 2021 law broadly blocks city and county officials in Texas’ urban areas from reducing or reallocating funds previously earmarked for law enforcement. Under the law, the county would be on the hook for the full cost of any contract deputies assigned to neighborhoods that cancel their patrol contracts 

Commissioner Adrian Garcia described the situation as a “textbook example” of unintended legislative consequences.

Though most commonly associated with the county’s eight elected constables, the sheriff’s office accounts for roughly 25% of the more-than 1,200 deputies assigned to contract patrol, according to budget documents. 

But even shifting positions between those nine law enforcement agencies is prohibited by the “defunding” law unless approved by voters, according to an August opinion issued by Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton.
