# The Philly Inquirer

Ian Austin, indicted for protesting inside a St. Paul, Minn. church, said he’s speaking out against fascism while he still can.

Ian Austin, like so many Americans, watched the video of an Immigration and Customs Enforcement agent fatally shooting Renee Nicole Good in January. But unlike most people, the 36-year-old U.S. Army veteran knows how it feels to shoot someone while wearing a uniform.

“I can still see myself on the firing line, and I can still feel what I felt in my stomach then. I couldn’t identify it at the time, but I knew it didn’t feel right,” Austin said. After watching the video, Austin drove from his home in Bryn Athyn to Minnesota, where he participated in a protest inside a St. Paul, Minn. , church.

He soon became a defendant in the high-profile federal case alleging protesters infringed on churchgoers’ rights. Former CNN anchor is one of the co defendants. To Austin, his trip to Minneapolis is part of a much longer journey that began during his military service in Afghanistan. Killing people at war, he said, traumatized him and led him to seek healing and spiritual renewal.

If convicted, he could face up to 10 years in prison, his attorney said, though she doesn’t expect that outcome. Defense attorneys in the case call it an unprecedented misuse of the Department of Justice to silence critics of the Trump administration’s immigration policies. The DOJ did not respond to requests to discuss the case. Austin’s story provides one glimpse into the lives of the 38 people now facing felony charges over the protest.

As ICE activity increased, so did opposition from members of the public. After Good was killed Jan. 7, protesters and federal law enforcement frequently clashed outside the Bishop Henry Whipple Federal Building, where people were being detained. That’s where Austin went first, and within a day, federal law enforcement detained him .

While in jail, Austin recalled, another protester told him about a planned protest at Cities Church in St. Paul, where one of the pastors was a local ICE official. The DOJ indictment says that inside the church, Austin was part of a group of “approximately 20-40 agitators” who were part of a “coordinated takeover-style attack and engaged in acts of oppression, intimidation, threats, interference, and physical obstruction. ” Protesters shouted “ICE Out! ” “Hands Up, Don’t Shoot!” and other chants, the document states, “while gesturing in an aggressive and hostile manner, which congregants and the pastor perceived as threats of violence and a potential prelude to a mass shooting. ”

Austin and others interfered with churchgoers’ ability to move freely, the indictment says, and Austin “approached the pastor and congregants in a menacing manner ... and loudly berated the pastor with questions about Christian nationalism and Christians wanting to have their faith be the law of the land.”

A slow-moving case Austin’s attorney, Sarah Gad, filed a motion to dismiss his case. She said her client’s actions did not violate the two federal laws he is charged under.

“For example, it’s not an offense to stand in a church and chant. And so it feels like the DOJ really stretched some preexisting federal laws to kind of turn this conduct of protesting at a church into a crime,” Gad said. Defendants are charged with violating the Civil Rights Act of 1871, enacted to protect Black people from the Ku Klux Klan and other violent white supremacist groups.

Gad noted that protesters are also charged with violating the Freedom of Access to Clinic Entrances Act — a law established to protect people entering abortion clinics, but which also includes a provision for those in places of worship. Last year the Trump administration pardoned 10 antiabortion protesters who were convicted of violating the same law when they blockaded a clinic in Washington, Gad noted.

This apparent double standard led her and colleagues to pen a “selective and vindictive prosecution” motion to dismiss, she said. With 38 defendants, the case, still in its pretrial phase, has moved slowly, Gad said. The defense has filed several more motions to dismiss, while the federal government has sought delays. At least five prosecuting attorneys have withdrawn from the case, court documents show.
