# Houston Chronicle 
Early in Donald Trump’s second term as president, federal immigration authorities added roughly 700,000 administrative warrants to a national crime database used by law enforcement agencies across the country, dragging local police, sheriffs and constables into Trump’s deportation campaign — whether they wanted to or not.

The civil warrants, which are not issued for criminal offenses and are not signed by a judge, do not give officers the authority to make arrests on their own. 

Their addition to the National Crime Information Center, or NCIC, the database used by virtually every law enforcement agency in the country, presents Texas police leaders with a difficult question: notify Immigration and Customs Enforcement when a warrant springs up, and risk alienating immigrant communities, or decline to honor the warrants and risk running afoul of state and federal officials. 

As part of an effort to identify how agencies are handling these administrative warrants, the Houston Chronicle reached out to dozens of police departments, sheriff's and constable’s offices in the Houston area and across Texas. Their responses, obtained through both the Texas Public Information Act and direct requests for comment, show a range of approaches — and confusion about what the warrants mean and how their officers should respond.
