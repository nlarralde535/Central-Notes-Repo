# Forbes
Key Facts

The Guardian on Tuesday published a report, citing government data, that showed ICE stopped recording data on miscarriages in October after 18 women lost their pregnancies during the first nine months of the Trump administration.

The Department of Homeland Security first told the outlet that while it wasn’t publishing data on lost pregnancies, it was tracking miscarriages in internal monthly reports, but later admitted even that data doesn’t go past October 2025.

The acknowledgement marks the first time DHS has ever admitted it lost count of miscarriages, even when U.S. senators asked the agency for the data, and comes less than a year after the American Civil Liberties Union called on the agency to address its systemic neglect of pregnant women.

In a statement to Forbes Tuesday, a Department of Homeland Security spokesperson said the report was “FALSE” and said the agency does track and report miscarriage data on a monthly basis, but did not respond to a request to share the information with a reporter. 

Key background

Under President Barack Obama in 2016, ICE issued policy guidelines creating a presumption against detaining pregnant women unless there were extreme criminal or national security threats. Trump ended that policy in his first term and the policy led to a sharp increase in the number of pregnant women in custody, drawing heavy backlash and resulting in congressional proposals like the Stop Shackling and Detaining Pregnant Women Act. Biden restored the policy in 2021 but the number of detained pregnant women surged again in Trump’s second term. And while DHS has historically submitted twice-yearly reports to Congress on the detention of pregnant people, those regular public disclosures stopped in 2025. Congressional Democrats and Senate committees have repeatedly pressed DHS for exact oversight metrics on maternal health, but because so many reporting requirements have been rescinded and other ways advocates used to collect information have been cut off—like direct monitoring inside detention centers—it’s hard to know exactly how many of these women are being held.
