# Votebeat
The U.S. Department of Justice late Thursday filed an unusual lawsuit against the state of Alaska seeking to block certification of its U.S. Senate race if the Republican incumbent loses.

With early voting underway in the contest that could determine partisan control of the Senate, and polls showing a tight race between incumbent U.S. Sen. Dan Sullivan and Democratic challenger Mary Peltola, the Trump administration is laying the groundwork to challenge the Alaska results in some circumstances

The lawsuit centers on the participation of a second candidate named Dan Sullivan on the ballot, something that the Justice Department said is likely to confuse Alaska voters.

Daniel J. Sullivan, a retired teacher and former member of the Alaskan Independence Party who switched his registration to Republican shortly before entering the Senate race, is one of four candidates for the seat.

Alaska election officials disqualified Daniel J. Sullivan from the ballot in June, but the former teacher challenged the decision. Sullivan rejected any suggestion he was working with Democrats to dilute the vote for Sen. Sullivan and said he ran because he was dissatisfied with the incumbent’s track record. The Alaska Supreme Court ultimately sided with the former teacher and cleared the way for him to be on the ballot.

Alaska’s ballot includes the word “incumbent” next to Sen. Sullivan’s name, but the Justice Department filing argues that the other Dan Sullivan’s presence on the ballot “threatens to mislead Alaskan citizens who want to vote for Senator Sullivan.” 

In such a case, the Justice Department wants a federal judge to stop election officials from certifying the election “pending an investigation to determine whether votes cast for Daniel J. Sullivan were meant to be cast for Senator Sullivan.”

Legal scholars said the lawsuit appears to have little chance of succeeding.

“It arises from real, valid, recognized concerns about a bad-faith sham candidate, potential confusion, and effective disenfranchisement,” Michael Morley, an election law expert at Florida State University said in an email. “But this particular lawsuit is frivolous and will be quickly dismissed.” 

Morley said “any concerns about the sham candidacy could have been raised before the election, before votes were cast,” and that the Senate itself could challenge the results and refuse to seat Peltola if she wins.

Ilya Somin, a constitutional law professor at George Mason University and scholar at the libertarian Cato Institute, also said the lawsuit appeared to be baseless.

“Any law suppressing candidates based on potential voter confusion would violate the First Amendment,” he said in an email.

“As to why DOJ waited so long, I don’t know,” he said. “Maybe this is one of those politically motivated cases they brought only after pressure from on high overruled line attorneys.”

President Donald Trump took credit for the lawsuit on Friday.

“I’m the one that said do it,” Trump told reporters.
