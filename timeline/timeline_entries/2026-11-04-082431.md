The measure drew strong reactions from supporters and opponents, with more than 56,000 voters casting ballots in favor, according to Armon Alex, director of the Fair Water Amendment campaign.

Alex said the results show residents want a different approach to how the city's water costs are shared.

"That's more than double the opposition votes against. That means nearly seven out of 10 voters said that Proposition B, the Fair Water Amendment, was something that they agreed in," Alex said.

Proposition B targets the industrial surcharge exemption program, which allows certain industrial water users to pay a fee in exchange for exemptions from drought-related surcharges.

Supporters argue the program gives large industrial users an advantage that residential customers and other water users do not have.

"We need to stop subsidizing billion-dollar corporations in Corpus Christi, and we need everyone to pay their fair share, and we all need to be better neighbors to each other," Alex said.

The campaign says the program will be phased out over the next five years. Alex said residents should be able to follow how the city carries out that transition.




Corpus Christi City Councilman Roland Barrera, who opposed Proposition B, said he believes the measure could face a legal challenge. He also questioned whether a citywide vote adequately represented the broader region's water customers.

"The citizens of Corpus Christi are the ones that voted on this referendum, and they only consume 13 percent of the water. That leaves out the people who utilize the water outside the city limits — Nueces County, Jim Wells, Bee County, Live Oak County, San Patricio County, Kleberg County. So none of those  consumers, ratepayers, had the opportunity to be able to vote on this particular item that affects people throughout the region," Barrera said.

Barrera also said the city will still need to find money for future water projects.

"The other thing is the purpose of the additional surcharge was to find solutions to keep us out of a drought, so now those funds still have to be expended," he said.




Dolly Gonzales Trolley, executive director of Voices for Coastal Bend Energy, also opposed the measure. She said industry contributions were intended to help support regional water infrastructure.

"It really meant turning away a voluntary fund that industry and our industry stakeholders here in the area were putting forward to build new water infrastructure in the region, and we felt it was important that the voters understood that, but the wording was very confusing for the voters," Gonzales Trolley said.

She said there could be legal challenges and questions about how the city will replace the funding.

"I think we could see some legal challenges to it, both from the industry stakeholders, or maybe at the state level, maybe even at the attorney general level. I'm not certain. I think there's a lot of decisions that could be forthcoming in the months to come, and there's a lot left to be decided," she said.

What happens next?

Alex said the campaign is prepared to defend Proposition B if it is challenged in court.

"We've got a team of litigators waiting to stand by. Whatever they're going to throw at us, we'll be ready to defend the people," Alex said.

He also cautioned that the amendment is not a complete answer to Corpus Christi's water challenges.
