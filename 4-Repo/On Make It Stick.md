# On the psychology of learning

Page xi
"The responsibility for learning rests with every individual." 
    ...I don't think I believe this. Individuals play a role (maybe even a primary role) in their own learning, but the responsibility to learn some shit cannot be said to rest exclusively with the individual. There'd be no need to compel children to go to school, or argue about what they should be taught, if that were truly so. 

Page 1 - 2
"Matt Brown was flying a twin-engine Cessna northeast out of Harlingen, Texas when he noticed a drop of oil pressure in his left engine. ... Matt has been messing around with piston engines since he was old enough to hold a wrench, and he knew he had a problem. ... his ability to work himself out of a jam illustrates what we mean in this book when we talk about learning: acquiring knowledge and skills and having them readily available from memory so you can make sense of future problems and opportunities."
    This ^ contradicts the previous quote, I think. Matt did not take responsibility for learning to work with piston engines from the time he could pick up a wrench, the people in his life at the time took responsibility to teach him and expose him. Yes, at some point he took that responsibility upon himself and furthered his knowledge/skills. But my point is proven: it takes a village, not a discrete set of individuals.

 - interleaving is studying/practicing multiple distinct subjects/skills

# Practical tips for learning shit

Page 16
The story of the psychology student going to his professor after gaining the first test of the semester contains another summary of the effective study strategies that will be explained in this book

Ineffective study methods: 
 - highlighting your own notes
 - rereading your highlighted notes
 - re-rereading the original material
Effective study methods: 
 - identifying the main ideas, and rephrasing them in your own words **while reading** thru the material first time
 -  explaining the main ideas in your own words, from memory later, **after reading** the material 
 - converting the main ideas into a series of questions, **while reading** thru the material first time
 - using these ^ self-generated questions to test yourself on the material later

***Low-stakes or no-stakes testing*** promotes retrieval of newly leaned information. The point of testing is not to "gauge learning" but to reinforce learning by inducing retrieval. The focus of the testee should be on retrieval, not on the consequences of potentially failing the test.

Page 88
***Reflection*** is the cognitive activity of taking time to review what has been learned from a given experience and asking yourself questions about it. Reflection can involve other cognitive activities liked to improved learning benefits: retrieval, elaboration, generation. 
After a lecture or reading assignment you might ask yourself: 
 - what are the key ideas? 
 - what are some examples? 
 - how do these relate to what I already know? 
After a session of practicing a new skill you might ask yourself: 
 - what went well? 
 - what could have gone better?

Page 158
In "The Takeaway" section if chapter 6, the author's summarize the key points related to "going beyond learning styles". These are good points that can be implemented in one's learning practice. 
 - *Be the one in charge*: go out and actively acquire the knowledge/skills you want, don't wait to be handed such
 - *Embrace the notion of successful intelligence*: "Consider your expertise to be in a state of continuing development", build up your strengths but also your weaknesses
 - *Adopt active learning strategies*: like retrieval practice, spacing, and interleaving. Don't assume you're doing something wrong just because the learning feels hard, or is not progressing as fast as you would like
 - *Distill the underlying principle, build the structure*: Break your subject down into its component parts. Which are the big ideas and which are supporting concepts and nuances?
