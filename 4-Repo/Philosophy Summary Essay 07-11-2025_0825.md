# The...

...jury appears to still be out when it comes to the subject of whence consciousness. As best as I can tell from our brief look into the matter, consciousness is something we all experience individually, and must take others at their word that they experience as well. Lots of smart people fling themselves at the task of figuring out why we are conscious, and locating the seat of conscious. I'm not convinced this work will bear much practical fruit, or will bear at least as much fruit as would bear inquiry into why we have 5 fingers: knowing the origin of the tools we have at our disposal does not change those tools in any way, we can learn how to leverage/take increasing advantage of them without needing to know where they came from, only that we have them. That being said, I'm not opposed to folks wasting their lives however they see fit. 

As for the subject of morality, there is much practical importance in establishing some kind of system of ethics, if for no other reason than that we might all be using the same words at the very least. In my opinion, the idea is not to come up with some all encompassing system of morality that provides explicit and precise answers to specific questions. This is not possible I don't think, if it were we would have such a system by this point. No, the idea is rather to assemble a set of tools and metrics for assessing whether a given practice or behavior or law (A) serves the purposes of, or is in line with some existing or imagined system of morality (B), which itself is simply a set of corresponding stated beliefs. The idea is to dispassionately assess the extent to which (A) serves and/or is consistent with (B).

Advocating for such a system makes me some combination of a Metaethical Coherentist, as well as a Moral Subjectivist. Metaethical Coherentism requires that moral beliefs "belong to a coherent system, [where] coherence is taken to involve three components: logical consistency, explanatory relations, and various inductive (non-explanatory) relations". The system holds itself up like a self-supporting reciprocal frame structure, and must do so because I do not believe there is any explicit moral foundation upon which any system can rest, the proof is left as an exercise. Moral Subjectivism says that each individual erects and maintains such a system within themselves, using pieces learned or borrowed from wherever, and the morality or immorality of ones actions depends on the extent to which said actions "cohere" with that internal system.  

To repeat, my hypothesis concerning metaethics is that morality "is" whatever we say it is, and what we say it is changes over time. A concrete example of this can be found in John Hasnas' paper The Myth of the Rule of Law. It is possible that the above-mentioned absence of any metaethical foundation is an artifact of our reliance on language to discuss morality and moral beliefs. As John Hasnas makes clear, neutral language impervious to interpretation is not possible to achieve, thus making discussions of morality as slippery as they have appeared to be for as long as we have been having them.

Hasnas' concept of the myth of the rule of law is characterized by an individual's "ability to maintain the belief that the law is a body of consistent, politically neutral rules that can be objectively applied by judges", as well as "the public's belief that the law consists of a set of definite and consistent 'neutral principles'  which the judge is obligated to apply in an objective manner, free from the influence of his or her personal political and moral beliefs." Hasnas claims that such a scenario does not exist. 

This myth arises due to the fallacious manner in which the flavor of reasoning employed for scientific/empirical endeavors is used to address legal questions. Hasnas refers to the fallacy of legal reasoning: inconsistent and contradictory laws permeate the legal landscape, meaning that within this domain, "a logically sound argument can be found for any legal conclusion", and a particular legal hypothesis having been shown to be correct does not imply that no other "correct" legal hypotheses may be arrived at through equally sound/valid reasoning: "as anyone who has studied a little logic can demonstrate, any conclusion can be validly derived from a set of contradictory premises."
 
Ultimately, what we have is a system of law that conforms to the range of ideological
opinion reflected by the individuals who make up the judiciary (and ultimately society at large) at any given moment in time. Hasnas asserts that what conclusion one finds will be determined by what conclusion one looks for, i.e., by the hypothesis one decides to test", and that these conclusions end up being those which are "most congruent with one's antecedent, underlying political and moral beliefs".  

I'll conclude with an 1897 quote from Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes, 
highlighted in Hasnas' paper. Justice Holmes is talking about judicial decisions, but I maintain that his words apply just as well to moral decisions in general:
```
The language of judicial decision is mainly the language of
logic. And the logical method and form flatter that longing for
certainty and for repose which is in every human mind. But
certainty generally is illusion, and repose is not the destiny of
man. Behind the logical form lies a judgment as to the relative
worth and importance of competing legislative grounds, often an
inarticulate and unconscious judgment, it is true, and yet the
very root and nerve of the whole proceeding. You can give any
conclusion a logical form
```
